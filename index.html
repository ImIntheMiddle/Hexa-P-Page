Abstract

Pedestrian path prediction is a safety-critical component of autonomous vehicles and social robots that must navigate alongside humans in open urban environments. In such deployments, predictions that imply unnatural accelerations or impossible turns can directly trigger unsafe planning decisions, especially when only a few past frames of the pedestrian are observed. Because pedestrians are full-body humans whose paths are governed by articulated locomotion, we exploit two resources: 3D human body pose, which encodes locomotion-level constraints, and a humanoid physics simulator, which synthesizes virtually unlimited, physically executable pose-and-path data. We propose “Physical Plausibility-aware Pre-training for Pedestrian Path Prediction” (Hexa-P), which uses this synthesized data to jointly pre-train a path predictor and a plausibility estimator. Unlike prior work that uses a separately trained plausibility estimator, this joint pre-training improves both prediction accuracy and physical plausibility. While this simulation involves only a single agent, multiple agents mutually influence one another's future paths in real-world scenes. We therefore introduce an adapter-based fine-tuning that preserves physical knowledge while adapting to scene-specific social path interactions. We also propose Target-relative Positional Encoding (TaPE) to bridge the gap between single-agent simulation and multi-agent path prediction. Experiments on JRDB, JTA, and the Urban dataset demonstrate accurate and plausible predictions of Hexa-P. Particularly in safety-critical short-observation scenarios, Hexa-P reduces prediction errors by up to 50% on the JRDB dataset compared with a strong pose-aware baseline.

Stage 1 · Pre-training in a physics simulator

Thousands of simulated humanoids teach Hexa‑P how a body walks

Recorded pedestrians only show paths that people happened to walk; they never show a path that a body cannot follow. Hexa-P therefore learns its motion prior in a physics simulator. In IsaacGym, a humanoid controller trained with reinforcement learning (PACER) walks randomly generated target paths, starting each episode from a motion-capture pose of AMASS. The simulator renders virtually unlimited, physically executable pose-and-path data, and Hexa-P pre-trains its Physical Core on it before it ever sees a real pedestrian.

Learning what a body can and cannot do

The simulator also shows which paths a body cannot follow. Each rollout earns a reward for tracking its path with a natural, energy-efficient gait, and a humanoid that falls earns little. The LocoVal-Head learns to predict this reward from the observed motion and a candidate path. After fine-tuning, the same head scores the paths that Hexa-P predicts for real pedestrians and filters out the implausible ones (LocoVal filtering).

Walks its target path. The walked path supervises the Path-Head, and the high reward tells the LocoVal-Head that this path is plausible.

Falls on a path with sudden turns. The rollout is left out of the path loss, and its low reward teaches the LocoVal-Head what an implausible path looks like.

Predicted paths that a humanoid can actually walk

Do the predictions obey the same physics? We hand the paths that the pre-trained Hexa-P predicts from the observed motion back to the simulated humanoid and let it walk them for 4.8 s. Over 2,048 rollouts it never falls and always ends within 0.5 m of the predicted goal, whereas paths extrapolated at constant velocity from the same observations make it fall in 59.3% of the rollouts.
